# C17 — Behavioral Rounds for Technical Roles

> **Day 6, Evening** · Track: FDE (applies to both) · ⭐⭐⭐
> **Why this exists:** both coverage maps were purely technical, and that was a gap. Every
> loop has one or two of these rounds, they are frequently where strong engineers get
> down-levelled, and they are far more preparable than people assume.

---

## The One Idea

**Behavioral rounds are technical rounds with a different surface.** Nobody is scoring your
warmth. They are scoring judgment under ambiguity, ownership, and whether you can be trusted
alone in front of a customer or a $400K training run.

The corollary: **a vague answer is a failing answer, not a safe one.** Candidates hedge into
generality thinking it's low-risk. It reads as "wasn't really there."

---

## 1. The structure that works

Forget the acronyms. Five beats:

1. **Situation, in two sentences.** Enough context to make the difficulty legible. No org charts.
2. **What made it actually hard.** The constraint, the tradeoff, the thing that made it a
   judgment call rather than execution. **If there was no hard part, it's not a story.**
3. **What *you* decided.** Not what the team did. What you specifically chose, and what you
   chose against.
4. **What happened.** With a number where one exists.
5. **What you'd do differently.** ← *This is where seniority shows.*

Beat 5 is the one people skip, and it's the highest-signal part. A candidate who can name the
flaw in their own successful project is demonstrating exactly the judgment the round exists to
measure. A candidate whose every story went perfectly is either not reflective or not senior.

Target: **two to three minutes.** Then stop and let them probe.

---

## 2. What each question is actually testing

| Question | What they're scoring |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about a project you're proud of" | Can you scope a story, and is the thing you're proud of the right thing to be proud of? |
| "Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone" | **The most diagnostic question in the loop.** Do you engage with the substance or with the person? Can you be wrong gracefully? |
| "Tell me about a failure" | Was there real cost, do you own it, did you learn something specific? |
| "A time you had to say no" | Can you protect scope without damaging a relationship? |
| "Something you shipped you weren't proud of" | Do you have a quality bar, and can you articulate the trade that made you ship anyway? |
| "How do you handle an unclear requirement?" | The scoping instinct (C13) in narrative form |
| "Tell me about a production incident" | Process under pressure. Do you stabilize before diagnosing? |
| "Why are you leaving?" | Whether you'll be a problem. **Never criticize your current employer.** |

---

## 3. The disagreement story, specifically

This question is where the most information transfers, so prepare it properly.

**A strong version has:** a real technical disagreement with real stakes; the other person's
position stated fairly and strongly (steelmanned — if you can't argue their side, you didn't
understand it); what evidence you brought; and an outcome where **either you changed your mind
or you didn't get your way and committed anyway.**

**What fails:** "I explained my reasoning and they came around." That's not a disagreement
story, it's a persuasion anecdote, and it tells the interviewer you may be someone who needs
to win.

**The strongest variant most people never tell:** a disagreement where you were wrong, you
found out, and you changed course. It costs nothing and signals more than any victory.

---

## 4. The failure story

Requirements: it must be a **real** failure, with **real** cost, that was **at least partly
yours**.

Disqualifying moves: a failure that's secretly a strength ("I cared too much about quality").
A failure caused entirely by someone else. A failure with no consequence. Anything where the
lesson is "communicate more" — too generic to be evidence of anything.

**The good shape:** here's what I believed, here's why it was reasonable at the time, here's
what I missed, here's what it cost, here's the specific practice I changed. The
*reasonable-at-the-time* part matters — it shows you can distinguish a bad decision from a bad
outcome, which is itself a senior distinction.

---

## 5. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it costs you |
|---|---|
| **"We" with no "I"** | The single most common down-levelling cause. They cannot assess you from team accomplishments. |
| Hero narrative | Everyone else was wrong, you saved it. Reads as poor collaborator, and it's rarely true. |
| No numbers | "Improved performance significantly" is not evidence. |
| Vagueness as safety | Sounds like you weren't really involved. |
| Blaming | Even when accurate, it's the wrong signal. |
| No reflection | "It went great" closes the highest-value part of the answer. |
| Rambling | Past ~4 minutes they've stopped listening and started worrying about your customer calls. |

---

## 6. Preparing properly

**Build a story bank of 6–8 stories, indexed by theme, not by chronology.** One story usually
serves three or four questions with different emphasis — the same incident can be your
disagreement story, your ambiguity story, and your incident-response story depending on which
beat you foreground.

Cover these themes: technical depth, disagreement, failure, ambiguity, saying no, an incident,
influencing without authority, and something you changed your mind about.

For each, write down the **numbers** in advance — latency before/after, cost, scale, headcount,
timeline. You will not produce them under pressure if you haven't.

Then **say them out loud.** Not reread — spoken. Written stories run long and sound written.

---

## Self-check

1. Tell your disagreement story in 2 minutes. Did you steelman the other side?
2. Does your failure story have a real cost attached, and is any of it yours?
3. Pick a project you're proud of. Now name its biggest flaw.
4. Count "we" versus "I" in your best story. Rebalance if needed.
5. Which of the eight themes do you currently have no story for?
